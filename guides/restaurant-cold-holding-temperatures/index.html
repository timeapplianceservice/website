Cold holding is the backbone of food safety in any kitchen. Equipment that cannot hold temperature puts food and your reputation at risk. This guide explains the common targets, how to monitor them and what to do when a unit drifts out of range.

In this guide

  • The numbers most kitchens work to
  • How to monitor temperatures
  • Prep tables and reach-ins need extra attention
  • What to do when a unit runs warm

The numbers most kitchens work to

Cold foods that need time and temperature control are generally held at 41°F (5°C) or below, and frozen foods are held frozen, commonly around 0°F. Your local health department and food code are the authority for your operation, so always follow their guidance.

Many operators set equipment a few degrees below the limit so that normal door openings and loading do not push product out of range.

How to monitor temperatures

  • Use a calibrated probe thermometer for product, not just the equipment display.
  • Check and record temperatures at opening, mid-shift and close, or as your program requires.
  • Place thermometers in different spots, including the warmest area such as near the door.
  • Consider continuous monitoring with alarms if you rely on a walk-in overnight.
  • Keep logs. They help you spot trends before they become failures.

Prep tables and reach-ins need extra attention

Prep tables hold food at the rail all day with the lid open, and reach-ins are opened constantly. Overfilled pans, blocked vents and dirty condensers all push temperatures up. Keep pans at the proper fill level, keep vents clear and have condensers cleaned regularly.

If a rail drifts above 41°F, move product to working refrigeration and call for service, rather than hoping it recovers during the lunch rush.

What to do when a unit runs warm

  • Confirm the reading with a probe thermometer.
  • Check that doors are closed and nothing is blocking airflow.
  • Move perishable product to working refrigeration if the unit remains above 41°F.
  • Note how long the unit was warm and what product was affected, then follow your health department guidance.
  • Call a technician promptly. A small problem is easier to fix than a failed unit.

Preventing temperature problems

Preventive maintenance, clean condensers, healthy gaskets and working fans are the best protection. Schedule maintenance ahead of summer, keep an eye on door seals and respond quickly to small drifts. Good habits in the kitchen, such as closing doors fully and not overloading shelves, help too.
